# Universal Assembly Language (UAL)

## Why UAL Came Up

Around 1988, my father suggested that if I wanted to learn assembly language, Universal Assembly Language (UAL) would be a good place to start.

UAL was attractive because it approached assembly-language concepts without immediately tying the student to all the peculiarities of one particular processor.

The important concepts include:

- registers

- memory

- instructions

- branching

- stacks

- the relationship between assembly instructions and machine operations

## Historical Lead

There was a specific Universal Assembly Language project in this period.

A 1984 technical report by I. Flores and N. G. Bourbakis was titled:

> *A Universal Assembly Language (UAL)*

There also appears to have been educational material using the name Universal Assembly Language for a machine-independent way of teaching assembly programming.

The exact UAL source my father encountered has not yet been identified.

## Why My Father's Recommendation Matters

My father was not simply recommending something he happened to see in a book.

He was an experienced programmer who had programmed directly in machine language — bits and bytes, including work involving punched codes — rather than merely programming in assembly language.

At the other end of the abstraction ladder, he was also familiar with high-level languages, including Ada and C++ and object-oriented programming.

He therefore understood programming at very different levels of abstraction.

His general philosophy was:

> Learn the language appropriate to the tool you are trying to build.

Given that background, he was particularly well positioned to recognize whether a supposedly universal assembly language captured the important underlying concepts.

He collected a great many books and would have encountered many technical ideas. His reaction to UAL was apparently something closer to:

> Now this is something smart.

## Why UAL May Still Be Worth Investigating

UAL is interesting today not necessarily because it is a language one would use to build modern software, but because its central educational idea remains valuable:

**Learn the underlying model first; learn the peculiarities of a particular processor second.**

That makes finding the particular UAL book or system my father encountered around 1988 worth pursuing.
