Yes. I think retaliation is worth developing as a separate theory, but the timing has to be handled carefully.

First, the first three-day notice has a concrete defect. California Courts says a three-day notice to pay rent or quit must state the exact amount of rent owed and that a notice demanding more than is actually owed is invalid. So if the amount due was nine hundred dollars and the first notice demanded nine hundred fifty dollars, that is significant. 

The second, corrected notice is different. Being only a few days late does not by itself make a three-day notice improper. California law allows a landlord to serve one once rent is actually past due. So the fact that Phil acted very quickly isn’t itself proof of retaliation. 

But your question is really about motive: why escalate immediately to formal notices when the amount involved was known, the delay was short, and you had communicated that you were trying to obtain the money?

California Civil Code section nineteen forty-two point five makes it unlawful in specified circumstances to try to force a tenant out in retaliation for protected complaints or for lawfully and peaceably exercising legal rights. Under subdivision d, the tenant has the burden of producing evidence that the landlord’s conduct was actually retaliatory.  California cases recognize that retaliatory motive can defeat an otherwise facially lawful attempt to recover possession. 

So the fact pattern I’d investigate is not merely, “Phil served me quickly.” It is the sequence of events.

You had been making complaints and asserting rights. Then, when you were briefly behind on rent, Phil rapidly resorted to formal eviction machinery. The first notice demanded the wrong amount. Instead of abandoning the effort after that error, another notice followed shortly afterward with the corrected amount. That sequence could be circumstantial evidence of motive, especially if Phil historically tolerated similar payment timing or treated other residents differently.

There is one important complication. Civil Code section nineteen forty-two point five subdivision a expressly refers to a tenant who “is not in default as to the payment of rent.” Because you were behind when these notices were served, I would not rely exclusively on that particular statutory presumption. Subdivision d is broader and prohibits retaliation for the lawful and peaceful exercise of rights, but there you need evidence of actual retaliatory purpose. 

So I think our next move should be evidentiary rather than theoretical: reconstruct the chronology immediately preceding the first three-day notice. What complaints did you make to Phil, Vince, APS, or anyone else, on what dates, and which of those did Phil know about before he served that notice?

That chronology could tell us whether “retaliation” is merely a suspicion or whether we have a persuasive circumstantial case.
