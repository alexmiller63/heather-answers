Yes. There is a potentially significant fair-housing issue here, but I would frame it somewhat differently from simply calling it an “ADA cross-complaint.”

For housing, the strongest statutes to examine are likely the federal Fair Housing Act and California’s Fair Employment and Housing Act, rather than relying solely on the ADA. Both protect people with disabilities in housing, and California expressly says that refusing a necessary reasonable accommodation in a housing rule, policy, practice, or service can constitute unlawful discrimination. 

Applied to what happened this morning, the factual sequence is unusually clean. You told Vince that you have medication requiring refrigeration, that you take it once a week, and asked permission to keep it in the locked refrigerator so it would not be stolen. He answered, “No.” California CRD specifically says an accommodation request does not have to use the words “reasonable accommodation”; the resident only needs to communicate the need for an adjustment because of a disability. It also says that if the provider cannot immediately grant the requested accommodation, the provider should engage in an interactive process to find an effective alternative. 

There is one weakness in the text as it presently stands, though: you didn’t tell Vince in that message that the medication is for diabetes or expressly connect the requested refrigerator access to a disability. That doesn’t necessarily defeat anything, particularly if they already know about your diabetes, but I would want the documentary record to establish that connection clearly before treating today’s “No” as a definitive denial of a disability accommodation.

And your screenshot is worth preserving. CRD actually tells people pursuing disability-related housing complaints to retain texts showing both the accommodation request and its denial. 

There is also a potentially important connection to an eventual eviction. California’s current CRD guidance specifically discusses reasonable accommodations in the eviction context and even says that accommodation requests can arise as late as an eviction trial in appropriate circumstances. 

But I would separate two questions before we build an unlawful-detainer strategy: first, whether these facts establish a fair-housing violation; and second, whether that violation can properly be pleaded as an affirmative defense, a cross-complaint in the unlawful detainer, or needs a separate civil action. Those are not necessarily the same answer.

The next thing I’d examine is California authority specifically on whether a FEHA/Fair Housing Act reasonable-accommodation claim can be brought as a cross-complaint in an unlawful detainer, because that’s the procedural question that could make this strategically useful rather than merely a separate discrimination claim.
