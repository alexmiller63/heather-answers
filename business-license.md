BUSINESS LICENSE

Hernandez Room and Board — San Bernardino

A potentially important issue concerns whether Phil had a valid City of San Bernardino business registration certificate covering the rental property at 3549 North D St., San Bernardino, CA 92405 when he demanded rent and served the 3-day notices.

I previously contacted the City of San Bernardino regarding Phil’s business registration. A City employee told me that Phil had not paid the required amount and that the City had been sending him demands for payment.

That telephone conversation should presently be treated as a factual lead. Before relying on it in court, it would be preferable to obtain City records documenting the status of the registration, the relevant dates, amounts owed, and any notices or demands sent to Phil.

SAN BERNARDINO MUNICIPAL CODE

San Bernardino Municipal Code § 5.04.525(F) governs business registration requirements for residential rental property.

The ordinance applies broadly to residential rental activity and includes rooming houses, lodging houses, boardinghouses, and single-family rental properties.

Most importantly, § 5.04.525(F)(6) provides that it is unlawful for a property owner, landlord, manager, or agent to demand, accept, receive, or retain payment of rent when the rental unit has not applied for and received the business registration certificate required by the section.

The City of San Bernardino’s own rental-property registration materials similarly state that it is unlawful to demand or receive rent for a property that has not been issued the required business registration certificate.

APPLICATION TO 3549 NORTH D ST.

The important factual question is not simply whether Hernandez Room and Board possessed some business registration somewhere in San Bernardino.

The important question is:

Was there a valid business registration certificate covering 3549 North D St. when Phil demanded the $900 rent and served the 3-day notices?

If the City confirms that the required certificate for this property was not valid at that time because Phil had failed to pay the required fees, the ordinance appears to create an important conflict:

1. Phil demanded $900 in rent.
2. Phil served a 3-day notice demanding payment of that rent.
3. The City ordinance may simultaneously have prohibited him from demanding, accepting, receiving, or retaining that rent.

POSSIBLE UNLAWFUL-DETAINER ISSUE

This creates a significant legal question requiring further research:

Can a landlord maintain a nonpayment unlawful-detainer action based upon a tenant’s failure to pay rent when a municipal ordinance expressly prohibited that landlord from demanding, accepting, receiving, or retaining the rent during the relevant period?

The existence of an expired or unpaid business registration should not yet be described as automatically defeating an unlawful-detainer action. California cases concerning other municipalities show that the consequences of licensing or registration violations can depend upon the precise language and purpose of the particular ordinance.

San Bernardino’s ordinance is potentially distinctive, however, because it does more than merely require a registration certificate. It expressly prohibits demanding or receiving rent without the required certificate.

EVIDENCE TO OBTAIN LATER

Useful City records could include:

• Business registration history for 3549 North D St.
• Expiration date of the last valid certificate.
• Whether required fees remained unpaid.
• Dates and contents of demands or notices the City sent to Phil.
• Applications or renewal applications submitted for the property.
• Penalties or enforcement actions associated with the delinquent registration.
• The property’s registration status on the dates of both 3-day notices.

Obtaining those records would convert the information received during the telephone conversation into documentary evidence.

WORKING THEORY

If Phil lacked the business registration certificate required for 3549 North D St., and San Bernardino Municipal Code § 5.04.525(F)(6) consequently prohibited him from demanding or receiving rent, there is a substantial legal question whether he could validly demand that same rent through a 3-day notice and use its nonpayment as the basis for an unlawful-detainer action.
