Yes. I checked the California authority, and there is an important correction to the strategy we were considering.

California’s general rule is that a defendant cannot maintain a cross-complaint or counterclaim inside an unlawful-detainer action. The California Supreme Court said this explicitly in Knowles v. Robinson and repeated it in Green v. Superior Court: because unlawful detainer is designed as a rapid proceeding to determine possession, a cross-complaint ordinarily cannot be used to inject an independent claim for affirmative relief. 

But that does not mean the fair-housing issue is excluded from the unlawful-detainer case. Quite the opposite. Green establishes the important distinction: although a cross-complaint is generally prohibited, a tenant may raise an affirmative defense that, if proven, would defeat the landlord’s right to possession.  California later applied that principle to retaliatory-eviction defenses as well. 

So, for your situation, I would think in terms of two tracks.

In the unlawful-detainer case, the reasonable-accommodation/disability-discrimination facts potentially belong in the Answer as affirmative defenses if they have a sufficient connection to the landlord’s claimed right to evict you. The objective there isn’t damages. It’s essentially: “You are not legally entitled to possession through this eviction because doing so under these circumstances violates applicable housing-discrimination protections.”

Separately, an FHA or California fair-housing cause of action seeking damages, injunctive relief, statutory remedies, or other affirmative relief could potentially be brought as its own civil action rather than as a cross-complaint in the UD. That’s significant because the California Court of Appeal reaffirmed quite recently in Duncan v. Kihagi that tenants can pursue affirmative relief unavailable in unlawful detainer through their own actions; they weren’t required to assert those claims as cross-complaints in the earlier UD proceedings. 

And there is a strategic benefit to that distinction: losing the ability to file a cross-complaint does not necessarily mean losing the underlying claim. California cases recognize that an unlawful-detainer proceeding doesn’t necessarily resolve all of the other disputes arising out of the landlord-tenant relationship. 

So I would revise our working theory from “ADA/FHA cross-complaint” to something more precise: a disability/fair-housing affirmative defense to the unlawful detainer, coupled, if warranted, with a separate FHA/FEHA civil action for affirmative relief.

There is one more question that I think is particularly important in your circumstances: whether a separate FHA/FEHA lawsuit could support an injunction or stay affecting the eviction while the discrimination claim is litigated. That could potentially accomplish some of what you originally hoped the cross-complaint would accomplish, but through the procedurally correct mechanism.

Yes. I looked specifically at whether the separate fair-housing case could be used to obtain an injunction affecting an eviction. The answer is potentially yes, but there is an important distinction between having authority to request an injunction and actually persuading a judge to stop an unlawful detainer.

The federal Fair Housing Act gives an aggrieved person a private civil action and expressly authorizes a court to issue a temporary injunction, temporary restraining order, permanent injunction, or other appropriate order when a discriminatory housing practice has occurred or is about to occur. 

California law is similarly strong. Government Code section twelve thousand nine hundred eighty-nine point one permits an aggrieved person to bring a separate civil action, even without first filing an administrative complaint. Section twelve thousand nine hundred eighty-nine point two expressly authorizes temporary and permanent injunctions and temporary restraining orders to prevent a defendant from engaging in or continuing an unlawful housing practice. 

That gives us a potentially useful structure for your situation. The unlawful detainer remains the landlord’s expedited possession case. You could raise any legally applicable disability-discrimination or reasonable-accommodation defenses there. Separately, a fair-housing plaintiff can seek affirmative relief, potentially including emergency injunctive relief. But merely filing that second lawsuit would not automatically stay the unlawful detainer. A court would actually have to issue appropriate injunctive relief.

There is also an important caution in California’s statute. Government Code section twelve thousand nine hundred fifty-five says, in its retaliation provision, that nothing there is intended to permit delay of an unlawful-detainer action.  So I would not build a strategy around the proposition that filing a FEHA case automatically slows down the UD. It doesn’t.

Your medication issue could nevertheless matter independently. California prohibits disability discrimination in housing,  and both California and federal law provide powerful injunctive remedies for proven or impending discriminatory housing practices. So if the facts ultimately supported a reasonable-accommodation violation connected to continued occupancy or an attempted eviction, there is statutory machinery for asking a court to intervene.

One especially interesting California case is DFEH v. 1105 Alta Loma Road. It involved disability discrimination and eviction proceedings. The history included unlawful-detainer actions, a writ of possession and ultimately an eviction; DFEH subsequently pursued disability-discrimination claims seeking damages and injunctive relief. That reinforces the point that the discrimination cause of action can exist independently of the UD proceeding. 

For your strategy, therefore, I would draw the map this way: the fair-housing claim is potentially a sword in a separate civil action and potentially a shield in the unlawful detainer, but we should not call the separate lawsuit an automatic mechanism for delaying eviction.
