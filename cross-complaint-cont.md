#Yes. I checked the California authority, and there is an important correction to the strategy we were considering.

California’s general rule is that a defendant cannot maintain a cross-complaint or counterclaim inside an unlawful-detainer action. The California Supreme Court said this explicitly in Knowles v. Robinson and repeated it in Green v. Superior Court: because unlawful detainer is designed as a rapid proceeding to determine possession, a cross-complaint ordinarily cannot be used to inject an independent claim for affirmative relief. 

But that does not mean the fair-housing issue is excluded from the unlawful-detainer case. Quite the opposite. Green establishes the important distinction: although a cross-complaint is generally prohibited, a tenant may raise an affirmative defense that, if proven, would defeat the landlord’s right to possession.  California later applied that principle to retaliatory-eviction defenses as well. 

So, for your situation, I would think in terms of two tracks.

In the unlawful-detainer case, the reasonable-accommodation/disability-discrimination facts potentially belong in the Answer as affirmative defenses if they have a sufficient connection to the landlord’s claimed right to evict you. The objective there isn’t damages. It’s essentially: “You are not legally entitled to possession through this eviction because doing so under these circumstances violates applicable housing-discrimination protections.”

Separately, an FHA or California fair-housing cause of action seeking damages, injunctive relief, statutory remedies, or other affirmative relief could potentially be brought as its own civil action rather than as a cross-complaint in the UD. That’s significant because the California Court of Appeal reaffirmed quite recently in Duncan v. Kihagi that tenants can pursue affirmative relief unavailable in unlawful detainer through their own actions; they weren’t required to assert those claims as cross-complaints in the earlier UD proceedings. 

And there is a strategic benefit to that distinction: losing the ability to file a cross-complaint does not necessarily mean losing the underlying claim. California cases recognize that an unlawful-detainer proceeding doesn’t necessarily resolve all of the other disputes arising out of the landlord-tenant relationship. 

So I would revise our working theory from “ADA/FHA cross-complaint” to something more precise: a disability/fair-housing affirmative defense to the unlawful detainer, coupled, if warranted, with a separate FHA/FEHA civil action for affirmative relief.

There is one more question that I think is particularly important in your circumstances: whether a separate FHA/FEHA lawsuit could support an injunction or stay affecting the eviction while the discrimination claim is litigated. That could potentially accomplish some of what you originally hoped the cross-complaint would accomplish, but through the procedurally correct mechanism.

Yes. I looked specifically at whether the separate fair-housing case could be used to obtain an injunction affecting an eviction. The answer is potentially yes, but there is an important distinction between having authority to request an injunction and actually persuading a judge to stop an unlawful detainer.

The federal Fair Housing Act gives an aggrieved person a private civil action and expressly authorizes a court to issue a temporary injunction, temporary restraining order, permanent injunction, or other appropriate order when a discriminatory housing practice has occurred or is about to occur. 

California law is similarly strong. Government Code section twelve thousand nine hundred eighty-nine point one permits an aggrieved person to bring a separate civil action, even without first filing an administrative complaint. Section twelve thousand nine hundred eighty-nine point two expressly authorizes temporary and permanent injunctions and temporary restraining orders to prevent a defendant from engaging in or continuing an unlawful housing practice. 

That gives us a potentially useful structure for your situation. The unlawful detainer remains the landlord’s expedited possession case. You could raise any legally applicable disability-discrimination or reasonable-accommodation defenses there. Separately, a fair-housing plaintiff can seek affirmative relief, potentially including emergency injunctive relief. But merely filing that second lawsuit would not automatically stay the unlawful detainer. A court would actually have to issue appropriate injunctive relief.

There is also an important caution in California’s statute. Government Code section twelve thousand nine hundred fifty-five says, in its retaliation provision, that nothing there is intended to permit delay of an unlawful-detainer action.  So I would not build a strategy around the proposition that filing a FEHA case automatically slows down the UD. It doesn’t.

Your medication issue could nevertheless matter independently. California prohibits disability discrimination in housing,  and both California and federal law provide powerful injunctive remedies for proven or impending discriminatory housing practices. So if the facts ultimately supported a reasonable-accommodation violation connected to continued occupancy or an attempted eviction, there is statutory machinery for asking a court to intervene.

One especially interesting California case is DFEH v. 1105 Alta Loma Road. It involved disability discrimination and eviction proceedings. The history included unlawful-detainer actions, a writ of possession and ultimately an eviction; DFEH subsequently pursued disability-discrimination claims seeking damages and injunctive relief. That reinforces the point that the discrimination cause of action can exist independently of the UD proceeding. 

For your strategy, therefore, I would draw the map this way: the fair-housing claim is potentially a sword in a separate civil action and potentially a shield in the unlawful detainer, but we should not call the separate lawsuit an automatic mechanism for delaying eviction.

Yes. Looking at the California and federal standards, I think the secure-refrigeration theory is legally plausible, although I haven’t found a published California case specifically about refrigerated diabetes medication.

The key is that fair-housing law does not require the accommodation to look like a traditional accessibility measure such as a ramp. California CRD defines a reasonable accommodation broadly as a change or exception to a housing provider’s rules, policies, practices, or services when necessary to give a person with a disability an equal opportunity to use and enjoy the dwelling. 

That fits the structure of what happened here quite well. The accommodation isn’t really “give me a refrigerator.” There is already a locked refrigerator. The requested accommodation is essentially:

Make an exception to the rule restricting my access to the locked refrigerator so that I can safely store medication needed for my diabetes.

That distinction matters. You’re requesting access to an existing housing service/facility for a disability-related purpose, rather than asking them to purchase equipment or undertake a substantial modification.

The elements

On the facts we presently have, I’d analyze the reasonable-accommodation claim this way:

1. Disability. Diabetes can qualify as a disability under fair-housing protections. California’s definition is particularly broad and includes physical conditions that limit a major life activity. 
2. Accommodation requested. You asked to keep medication requiring refrigeration in the locked refrigerator. That’s a request for an exception/change concerning use of an existing facility.
3. Disability-related necessity. This is probably the element where documentation matters most. We’d want evidence establishing that the medication is for diabetes and what storage conditions the medication requires. Your follow-up—“It’s my diabetes medication.”—now expressly establishes the disability/medication connection in the conversation.
4. Reasonableness. On the facts you’ve described, allowing one medication to occupy some space in an already-existing locked refrigerator appears facially modest. A provider can resist an accommodation that creates an undue financial/administrative burden or fundamentally alters its operation, but CRD recognizes those as limitations on the accommodation obligation. 
5. Refusal/interactive process. The response “No” is important. California CRD says that when the requested accommodation cannot immediately be granted, the provider should engage in a timely, good-faith interactive process to identify an effective accommodation.  CRD recently reiterated that failing to engage with a tenant concerning a reasonable-accommodation request can itself implicate FEHA. 

There’s an important factual qualification: Mounjaro’s exact storage requirements matter. We shouldn’t build the legal argument around an assumption that it must continuously remain refrigerated without checking the manufacturer’s current instructions and the circumstances of your particular medication. The stronger proposition is that you need a medically appropriate and secure way to store your diabetes medication.

What I find particularly significant

The law talks about equal opportunity to “use and enjoy” the dwelling. That isn’t limited to physically entering the property. Disability Rights California likewise describes accommodations as changes necessary to provide a disabled person an equal opportunity to use and enjoy housing. 

Being able to keep necessary medication safely where you live is a credible candidate for that protection.

And the locked aspect makes the facts more interesting. Your request isn’t merely for refrigeration—you’ve explained that you’re concerned about the medication being stolen. So the potential accommodation is secure refrigerated medication storage using a locked refrigerator that already exists.

I would therefore rate the theory as substantial enough to preserve and investigate, but not yet something I’d characterize as a proven violation.

The next piece I would investigate is extremely concrete: Mounjaro’s official storage requirements and whether the medical-storage facts establish “necessity” strongly enough for the fair-housing accommodation analysis. That could either strengthen this theory considerably or expose a weakness before we rely on it.

fifth answer

In a legal document, Alexander, I would avoid arguing that “everyone knows” GLP-1 medications require refrigeration. Legal writing works better when we turn that intuition into a sequence of provable facts and then draw the conclusion.

For example, in an affirmative defense or declaration, the argument could look like this:

Defendant has diabetes and is prescribed Mounjaro (tirzepatide) for treatment of that condition. Defendant receives the medication in quantities of 4 single-dose pens, representing approximately 28 days of treatment. The manufacturer’s storage instructions provide that Mounjaro should be stored under refrigeration at 36°F–46°F (2°C–8°C), although an individual pen may remain at room temperature for no more than 21 days. Consequently, room-temperature storage does not provide an appropriate means of storing Defendant’s entire 4-dose supply.

Defendant therefore requested permission to store the medication in the residence’s existing locked refrigerator. The requested accommodation required no new refrigerator, construction, or significant expenditure; Defendant requested only permission to use existing secure refrigerated space for his diabetes medication.

The housing provider refused the request. After the refusal, Defendant expressly informed the housing provider that the medication was his diabetes medication.

The requested accommodation was reasonable and necessary to permit Defendant to store medication used to manage his disability under the manufacturer’s labeled storage conditions. The refusal therefore constitutes a failure to provide a reasonable accommodation as required by applicable fair-housing law.

Notice what that does. We don’t have to prove that Vince should have known anything about GLP-1 drugs. We establish diabetes → prescribed medication → 4-dose supply → manufacturer’s refrigeration instructions → 21-day limitation → existing locked refrigerator → very small accommodation requested → refusal.

There’s another useful legal-writing technique here. If we eventually attach the manufacturer’s instructions as an exhibit, the document can say something like “A true and correct copy of the manufacturer’s storage instructions is attached as Exhibit __.” Then we’re not asking the judge to take our word for the refrigeration requirement.

And I would distinguish between what you personally testify to and what an attorney might put into a memorandum of points and authorities. Your declaration establishes things such as “I have diabetes,” “I receive 4 pens,” “I asked Vince,” and “Vince said no.” The legal brief then takes those facts and applies the FHA/FEHA reasonable-accommodation standards to them.

That division—evidence first, legal conclusion second—makes the argument much stronger than “everybody knows Mounjaro needs refrigeration.”
