HOUSING CONDITIONS AND OCCUPANCY — BRICK TESTING

This note collects preliminary brainstorming concerning bathroom access, sanitation, occupancy, and the legal classification of the house at 3549 North D St., San Bernardino.

The purpose at this stage is to identify and test potential legal issues. It is not to make complaints to enforcement agencies or take actions that could unintentionally cause other residents to lose their housing.

THE PHYSICAL SITUATION

Approximately 15 adults live in the house.

The property has 2 bathrooms.

The larger bathroom is the bathroom normally used by the residents. It is also the bathroom where residents take their showers.

The second, smaller bathroom is located inside Vince’s room. It contains a shower, but as far as I know, no one uses that shower.

Residents generally have to ask Vince’s permission to use the bathroom located in his room.

Therefore, although the building physically contains 2 bathrooms, there is a legitimate question whether both bathrooms can properly be considered available to approximately 15 residents.

In practical terms, approximately 15 residents may be relying primarily upon 1 freely accessible bathroom for toilet use and bathing.

THE PORTA-POTTY

Bathroom access was discussed during the APS meeting.

Phil said that if the residents wanted a porta-potty, he could have one at the house by the next day.

I subsequently told Phil that the residents did want a porta-potty.

The porta-potty was not provided.

Phil’s willingness to propose an additional toilet is potentially relevant because it indicates that bathroom access was a sufficiently concrete concern for him to propose an immediate solution.

CALIFORNIA SANITATION REQUIREMENTS

California law recognizes inadequate sanitation as a condition that can cause a residential building to be considered substandard.

Potential sanitation problems include inadequate or insufficient toilets, lavatories, bathtubs, and showers.

However, the precise number of plumbing fixtures legally required depends upon the property’s legal occupancy and use classification.

Therefore, it would be premature to assert simply that 15 residents legally require a particular number of toilets or showers without first determining the classification applicable to this property.

THE AVAILABILITY QUESTION

There may be an issue separate from simply counting plumbing fixtures.

The house physically contains 2 toilets, but one is located inside Vince’s room and residents generally must obtain his permission to use it.

This raises the question:

Does a bathroom count as a bathroom available to residents when it is located inside the house manager’s private room and residents generally require his permission to use it?

That question deserves further legal research.

PROPERTY CLASSIFICATION

Publicly available property information describes 3549 North D St. as a 4-bedroom, 2-bath single-family residence.

It is apparently being used to house approximately 15 adults as part of Hernandez Room and Board.

This creates several related questions:

What is the property’s legally permitted occupancy classification?

Is its actual operation properly classified as a single-family rental, boarding house, rooming house, lodging facility, group living arrangement, or some other category?

How many residents is the property legally permitted to accommodate?

How many toilets, sinks, showers, and bathing facilities are required for that classification and number of residents?

Does the bathroom inside Vince’s room count toward those requirements if residents do not have unrestricted access to it?

These questions should be researched before reaching conclusions.

POSSIBLE LARGER OCCUPANCY ISSUE

There may be a larger issue beneath the bathroom question:

Is the use of a 4-bedroom single-family property to house approximately 15 room-and-board residents legally permitted at all?

That could potentially become an important issue.

However, it also presents a serious strategic concern.

PROTECTING THE OTHER RESIDENTS

I do not want legal research concerning Phil or my own potential eviction to result in approximately 15 residents suddenly losing their housing.

If the City’s zoning, building, occupancy, or safety requirements are being violated, making a complaint or requesting an inspection could potentially produce consequences affecting residents who have nothing to do with my dispute with Phil.

Therefore, discovering a possible violation does not mean that it should immediately be reported to Code Enforcement or another agency.

For now, the appropriate approach is research rather than enforcement.

We can determine what laws apply, understand whether the property appears to comply with them, and preserve potentially relevant information without automatically triggering government action against the house.

This distinction is particularly important because information about an occupancy or sanitation violation might potentially be useful defensively without requiring me to initiate an enforcement proceeding that could harm other residents.

WORKING PRINCIPLE

The guiding principle for this part of the research is:

“We can examine the brick without throwing it through the window.”

In other words, every potential legal issue can be investigated and tested. But discovering a useful legal issue does not mean that it should automatically be used in a way that creates unnecessary consequences for other people.

The immediate next step is therefore to continue researching the applicable occupancy and plumbing requirements without making an enforcement complaint.
